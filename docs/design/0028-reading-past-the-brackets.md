# Reading past the brackets

Some text wraps a whole phrase in brackets. A transcript of an interface reads `<Log in> <Register>`; a Chinese novel wraps its headings in `【】` and its titles in `《》`. Read aloud exactly as written, a voice says the punctuation too, and the result is not something anyone listens to twice.

So the owner can ask for the outer brackets not to be spoken. It is on from the start. An owner who wanted the brackets read aloud would be a strange one to design for, and the owner's desktop copy already does this — the same book read in both places should be read the same way.

Only the outer layer of a group goes, and only when the whole passage is bracket groups with nothing but punctuation and spacing between them. Anything less certain is left exactly as written. Saying two extra characters out loud is a nuisance; deleting a word the author wrote is a lie about the book, and the second is much worse than the first.

## The list of brackets is the owner's

The starting list is the angle brackets and the square brackets. That list is Western, and this app is read in Chinese, where the brackets that actually wrap a heading are different characters entirely. A fixed list would mean the setting does nothing on the books it was asked for, so the owner can change it.

Changing it works the way the rest of the app works when a setting guards something: while the removal is switched on, the list is fixed and cannot be typed into. The owner switches it off, edits the list, and switches it back on — and it is that last action, not each keystroke, that checks the list and puts it to work. This is the same shape as a speech provider's settings, which are likewise frozen while it is switched on.

A list that does not make sense is refused at that moment, and the refusal says which entry is the problem and offers to go back to the starting list. It is said in place, under the setting, rather than in a box that interrupts. A mistyped bracket is a small mistake and does not deserve a bigger interruption than the mistake itself.

## What it costs

The audio for a passage is the audio of what was actually said. So when the owner changes what is said — by switching the removal on or off, or by changing the list — the passages already saved on the device are the audio of the old answer, and the app will not play them for the new one.

That is deliberate, and the alternative is worse. Reusing the old audio would mean the word being highlighted and the word being spoken drift apart, which is the one failure this app is built to prevent. So a change to this setting means a chapter already downloaded has to be downloaded again before it is heard the new way. The app says so under the setting rather than interrupting with it, because this is a switch that gets used about once.

The audio of the old answer is not thrown away either. Switching back makes it playable again immediately, so deleting it automatically would be spending the owner's money to re-download something they already had. It stays on the device and keeps counting towards the space the app reports — which would otherwise leave the owner looking at megabytes saved, no chapters downloaded, and no way to reconcile the two. Managing downloads therefore offers to remove everything saved for a document, so the space is both visible and recoverable without anyone guessing what the owner meant.

## What was turned down

**A fixed list of brackets.** Much simpler, and it is what the desktop started with. It was turned down because the starting list contains nothing that appears in a Chinese book, so for this owner the setting would have been an switch that does nothing.

**Keeping the old audio and adjusting it.** This would avoid re-downloading anything. It was turned down because it requires the app to work out, every time it plays a saved passage, which answer that passage was saved under — and if it ever gets that wrong, nothing announces it. The highlight simply sits on the wrong word. Paying to download a chapter again is a cost the owner can see and decide about; a highlight quietly landing one word out is not.

**Deleting the old audio when the setting changes, or refusing to change the setting until the downloads are cleared.** Both keep the device tidy. Both were turned down for the same reason: they spend the owner's downloads to tidy up after a switch that is flipped once, and the second makes a setting refuse to change because of something that has nothing to do with it.
