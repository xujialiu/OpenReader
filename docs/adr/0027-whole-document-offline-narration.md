# Explicit offline narration outlives the reader

The product agreement is [design 0027](../design/0027-whole-document-offline-narration.md). Ordinary playback remains memory-only; only explicitly selected download tasks persist audio. This extends ADR 0002's deferred whole-document preparation without introducing automatic disk caching of everything heard.

## One extraction and one synthesis identity

Chapter names and hierarchy are read from the EPUB container, OPF and EPUB 3 navigation or EPUB 2 NCX through archive range reads. This does not inflate chapter bodies, render the book or synthesize speech. A metadata-only plan is sufficient for selection; it is not a claim that speech text or whole-document coverage is already known.

After Download selected, the scheduler asks an isolated EPUB rendition to prepare the selected chapter's spine file using the exact computed-style DOM walk in `highlighter.ts`. It then synthesizes that chapter, and while it does, and only while the app is in the foreground, prepares the chapters it will take next (amendment of 2026-09-28, #76). Chapters sharing a file reuse its one extraction; selecting a late chapter never requires rendering all earlier sections. The rendition stays mounted across requests for the same active document, with a separate ReaderProvider and renderer cache directory, so preparation cannot move the visible reading or replace its HTML. Navigation anchors divide the resulting utterances into chapters; they do not change sentence boundaries. `segmentBlocks` and `splitWithSentencex` are shared with playback. A separate XML body-text extractor remains rejected because authored `display` rules decide block boundaries and hidden text; a different sentence would miss the saved clip even if the interface claimed the chapter was complete.

The first implementation published the chapter list only after extracting every spine file. The owner's long-book screenshot showed an empty selector at 624 sections read while Contents was already usable. Issue #6 removes that dependency: navigation metadata is read first, and body preparation belongs to the requested download rather than opening its controls. Metadata includes all unlisted spine files and possible text before a leading fragment; empty parts are excluded once actually inspected. Whole-document completion requires every section to have been prepared and all its speakable text saved, never merely all presently prepared rows.

The isolated Reader must also disable the library's independent location scan. Its installed template starts `book.locations.generate(1600)` unless `initialLocations` is truthy, even when `waitForLocationsReady` is false. That generator loads every linear spine member; looking only at our `preparedSections` would miss this unrelated work. The published `initialLocations` type is `ePubCfi[]`, but `useInjectWebViewVariables` actually interpolates JavaScript source without JSON serialization: an empty array emits `const initialLocations = ;`, while the source string `'[]'` emits a valid empty array and takes `book.locations.load([])`. The preparation-only adapter supplies that stable source value through an explicit type boundary. A regression test executes the actual installed template's initialization branch and checks that `load([])` runs and `generate()` does not. The visible reader is unchanged.

The clip key remains provider, voice and exact utterance text, independent of playback speed. Documents own their saved clips and deletion. Preparation and playback share a bounded memory store and an in-flight synthesis map. Persistent audio is consulted before credentials or enabled-provider checks, so already-paid audio does not become unusable when a provider is disabled or a key is removed.

## Persistence and lifecycle

The SQLite catalog stores tasks, chapter metadata, prepared chapter text and completed audio records separately, so preparing a chapter does not rewrite all earlier text. Chapter text and its prepared marker commit in one transaction. Each audio payload is committed before its ready database record, which records actual byte length and supplied word timings. A recovery sidecar is written atomically; the app-owned offline directory is excluded from backup. PCM is stored as Apple Lossless, preserving sample counts and word-timing alignment rather than introducing an encoder-delay correction. Already encoded provider audio is preserved. The non-iOS fallback compresses PCM losslessly with gzip; native Android background execution is not provided by this iOS module. The earlier JSON plan/task formats are intentionally discarded under the unreleased-data reset described below.

One scheduler prepares documents in insertion order, goes on while a Reading plays (#75, below), and retains completed clips on pause, interruption and restart. Bounded retry applies to transient failures; authentication, quota and storage failures require explicit continuation. Missing playback audio blocks the queue instead of skipping unheard text. Explicit Play retries the missing utterance.

iOS uses a bounded UIApplication background task and an NWPathMonitor. Expiration marks preparation interrupted, unless a Reading plays (#75, below), and retains its progress; returning to the foreground resumes it. No silent audio is used to extend execution. This does not promise that a whole long document finishes while the app stays backgrounded or after a force quit.

## Display names

Explicit names are recorded in `display-names.json`, keyed by Document Id, and shown in the existing catalogue title. EPUB metadata cannot overwrite an explicit name. The shared catalogue format gains no fields, following ADR 0003's sibling-file rule; neither document identity nor the original EPUB is changed.

## SQLite owns offline metadata; audio keeps its file identity

Issue #7 measured the legacy owner plan at 131,686 texts and a bounded 100 cold clip-size checks at 508.741 ms. `hasSavedVoice` performed that scan synchronously during Reader rendering; replacing only that check in the debugger let the same EPUB open. The owner approved SQLite rather than a custom JSON inventory. Indexed document/voice queries replace render-time hashes and file checks; chapter metadata and text are queried separately, and task state is persisted in the same database. Library, reading positions and credentials retain their existing stores.

Audio is separated into document/voice directories, with a voice-independent hash of the exact text as the filename key. That same text key is stored in chapter membership once, in the transaction that saves prepared text. Progress joins membership with the selected voice's indexed clips, so requesting a second voice never loads or re-hashes other prepared chapters. A durable write intent precedes file creation; audio and its recovery sidecar are stored before committing the ready clip record. Deletion records its intent before removing files, including cancellation of in-flight writes. Recovery handles those intents without marking an absent payload ready. SQLite transactions cannot atomically include filesystem operations, so an audio filename alone is not the completion record.

The owner subsequently waived legacy compatibility because the app is unreleased and authorized deletion of the current downloads. Initialization removes only the previous `offline-narration` directory and creates the SQLite-backed `offline-narration-v2` store. There is no legacy plan/task importer or playback fallback. EPUB files, Library, reading positions and credentials are outside the reset. New recovery sidecars serve interrupted writes only; they are not scanned to infer availability. Async queries feed cached React snapshots; normal chapter queries omit text and the scheduler loads selected chapter text on demand. Neither render nor a memoized render callback performs database or filesystem work.

A correction from #15. In expo-file-system 57.0.7 `File.move()` is a native `AsyncFunction` that returns a promise and `moveSync()` is the synchronous form (`ios/FileSystemModule.swift:238,242`); `write`, `delete` and `create` are synchronous. `saveClip` called `move` without awaiting it, wrote the sidecar's `size` from `target.size` on the next line, and its `finally` then deleted the pending payload if it was still there, so a move that had not yet run had nothing left to move. On the iPhone 16 simulator the first clip written into a new document/voice directory was twice left as a sidecar with `size: null` and no payload, and the repository answered "The saved audio could not be verified."; that later clips of the same run were fine was observed, not measured. The `null` comes from the library, not from us: a missing file's `size` is `null` on both platforms (iOS `try? file.size` over `attributesOfItem`; Android `size: Long?`, `null` unless `file.exists()`), where the type declaration says `number` and the documentation says `0`. The payload and `writeJson`'s non-native fallback now move with `moveSync`, as `library.ts` already did, so each file is under its final name, with its real size, before the next line reads it. The test double's `move` settles on a later task, as the installed one does; its synchronous `move` was why the suite never saw this.

## The catalogue decides what is played from disk, never whether anything is played

Two corrections after the review that opened #13 and #14. `synthesize` in `runtime.ts` asks for saved audio through `savedClip`, whose rejection is reported once as the store error and answered as a miss, so a database written by a newer build (`user_version` above 1), a sidecar that does not parse or an open error in the directory stops downloads, inventory and progress but not the reading, which goes on over the network as if nothing were saved. A sidecar that fails `JSON.parse` is treated as audio that was not saved: `lookup` returns null and recovery discards the pending write with its files. Before this, `offlineRepository()` was awaited unconditionally on the first line of every synthesis, and a failing store made every utterance fail, online, for every document.

Cleanup is batched and off the open path. `recover()` settles pending writes, which are few and needed for correctness, then starts `cleanDeletions` in the background and exposes it as `cleanup`; reads already exclude `state='deleting'`, so a row waiting for cleanup is invisible. A document removal is recorded in a `removals` table in the marking transaction and finished by deleting the directory and then its rows in one transaction; a chapter deletion removes files first and drops rows in one statement per 500 keys through `json_each`, files before rows so that a crash between them leaves only rows that the next cleanup drops. Counted with the test adapter before the change: one saved clip is 3 exclusive transactions, `removeDocument` of 1,000 clips 1,001 transactions and 1,000 per-clip file removals before a directory delete that made them redundant, and `recover()` after an interrupted removal 1,000 transactions before the repository resolved. After: 2 transactions and one directory removal for a document of any size, and 1 + ⌈n / 500⌉ for n chapter clips. The count is the measure because expo-sqlite 57 opens a new native connection for every `withExclusiveTransactionAsync`; device time was not measured. The scheduler also asks `progress` once per run for the chapters already complete and skips them, so resuming a long task no longer re-checks every saved text. `removals` is additive and `user_version` stays 1.

## The task names the chapter it is on, and the ring is two drawings

The drawer's ring (#38) spins only while the text of the chapter being written is being counted, the one moment its fraction does not exist; a chapter whose text is prepared ahead of its turn (#76) does not spin, because its ring is empty either way. Which chapter is being written cannot be read off the task's own list: the scheduler walks `task.chapters` in the order the owner tapped them, skips whatever `completed()` returned at the start of the pass, and passes over a prepared chapter with no text without ever marking it complete, because `progress` counts `prepared AND text_count>0 AND COUNT(c.key)=ch.text_count` as complete. The first unfinished chapter in task order is therefore not always the one being worked on. The scheduler writes `task.current` before it prepares or fetches a chapter, and clears it when a pass starts and when it ends. It is saved with the rest of the task, as one JSON value in the catalogue's `state` table, so a task that was being written when the app was killed comes back from `startDownloads` as `queued` still naming that chapter, and stays so until a pass on it begins, behind another document's download or for as long as the scheduler is held back (`allowed()` is false). The drawer therefore reads it only in `preparing` and `downloading` (`WRITING` in `download-rows.ts`), where it is either null or the chapter being worked on. The first version read it in every state that goes on by itself, `queued` and `waiting` included, and after a restart put the Manage ring on a chapter nothing was writing, which then could not be selected for deletion; that was found by reading the restore path, not on the device.

The row rule is a pure module, `src/app/download-rows.ts`, so the suite checks it without rendering, which `test/README.md` rules out for components. The same rule with `manage` set is the Manage downloads list (#37): a ring on `task.current` while the task is preparing or downloading, a checkbox on anything with a saved clip, `null` otherwise, and `listedInManage` lists a heading when any chapter under it has a marker. The link is rendered while `occupied(document)` is positive or a task for the document is not `done`.

The ring is `react-native-svg`: the track and the glyph in one `Svg`, and the arc in a second one on an absolutely positioned `Animated.View`, because the indeterminate state is a quarter arc turned by `Animated.loop` on the native driver and turning the whole drawing would turn the square with it. The arc is a `strokeDasharray` of `fraction × 2πr` followed by the circumference on a circle rotated −90°, so that it starts at twelve o'clock, where the HIG's clockwise fill starts. Pause and Continue are `toggleTask`, the same call the removed link made; a ring on a paused, blocked or interrupted task shows the triangle and continues, one on a queued, preparing, downloading or waiting task shows the square and pauses. The rows of a task in those last four states were already excluded from selection; the three halted states were not, and now are, so a paused chapter is resumed by its ring rather than re-selected.

## Amendment (2026-09-23, #45)

"Explicit Play retries the missing utterance" above was literally one utterance: `play()` took the cursor's out of the failed set and left the rest, while the read-ahead refuses two at a time, so one network failure cost a press of Play per sentence in flight, and each stop showed the refusal from before the press (`notes/NOTES_2026-09-23.md`, 13:30). Explicit Play now retries every missing utterance: `retryOnPlay` in `src/playback/read-ahead.ts` empties the failed set, moving the queue back to the cursor only when the cursor's own utterance is missing, and `play()` clears the last refusal with it. Missing audio still blocks the queue, nothing is retried without that press, and a stop after it names a failure that happened after it.

## Amendment (2026-09-24, #56): a ring pauses its chapter, and chapters go in the list's order

Two sentences above no longer hold. "The scheduler walks `task.chapters` in the order the owner tapped them" and "Pause and Continue are `toggleTask` … a ring on a paused, blocked or interrupted task shows the triangle and continues, one on a queued, preparing, downloading or waiting task shows the square and pauses" described a download with one switch: every ring handed the document's one task for the voice to `toggleTask`, so a tap on the chapter being written stopped every chapter. The owner asked on 2026-09-24 for a ring to pause its own chapter, for the next chapter waiting its turn to start, and for Pause all where Manage downloads offers Delete all saved audio (design 0027).

The task keeps one state and gains `paused?: string[]`, the chapters the owner paused, beside `failed`. The rules are a pure module, `src/offline/pausing.ts`. `tapChapter` toggles one chapter in the set while the task goes on by itself (`GOES_ON`: queued, preparing, downloading, waiting), and makes the task `paused` when every chapter left is in the set; `paused` therefore means that, and nothing else. On a `paused` task it rebuilds the set from the chapters left without the tapped one and queues the task, which also covers a task stored as `paused` before the set existed. On `blocked` and `interrupted`, which stop the whole download by themselves, it continues the task as `toggleTask` used to (state `queued`, error and `failed` cleared) and takes the tapped chapter out of the set. `pauseAll` puts every chapter not failed in the set; `resumeAll` empties it and clears `failed`, which keeps Retry failed, the same call on a `done` task. `goesOn` decides between the two labels and the runtime's `toggleTask` between the two actions, so they cannot disagree. "Left" excludes chapters complete for the voice; the runtime reads them from the drawer's progress snapshot (`chapterProgress` for the task's voice), which the drawer requests while it is open, the only time a ring or Pause all can be tapped. Without it a complete chapter would count as left, which only delays `paused` until the scheduler's next pass.

The scheduler no longer walks `task.chapters`. Each time it needs a chapter it re-reads the plan and takes the first chapter of the plan, which is the list's order, that is in the task and not finished, failed or paused. `finished` is `completed()` plus every chapter whose texts all went through in this pass, including a prepared chapter with no text, which `progress` never counts complete: without the set the scheduler would choose it again forever. Every await that checked the task is still active and still holds the chapter now also checks the chapter is not paused (`here()`), so a chapter paused mid-write is left at the next await and the next one starts. Its in-flight clip is kept, because the runtime's `wanted()` in `fetch` looks at membership and deletion epochs, not at `paused`. A request that fails after its chapter was paused records nothing against that chapter; a key or quota failure is met again by the next chapter. When only paused chapters are left the pass ends with the task `paused`, and the next document's queued download has its turn. The chapters left are kept in sets: Select all on the owner's long book makes a task of 2,077 chapters (notes/NOTES_2026-09-20.md), and `includes` inside a walk over the plan would be quadratic on every choice.

Reading the old loop for this found one more fault, not seen on the device: after `load`, a chapter removed from the task by a deletion made the loop `break`, which ended the pass and marked the task `done` with its other chapters unwritten. It now moves on to the next chapter.

`enqueue` leaves the set alone, so adding chapters never resumes the paused ones, and it takes out a chapter chosen again. `deleteDownloaded` removes deleted chapters from it, and `persist` copies it. `startDownloads` needed nothing: it never touched `paused` tasks, and the set is saved with the task's JSON. The row rule draws a ring halted when `HALTED` includes the task's state or its own chapter is paused. The drawer puts Pause all or Resume all opposite Manage downloads in the download view, and the task line is not drawn for a `paused` task, whose state the control already says; `pauseAll` and `tapChapter` clear the error when they pause, so a waiting task's network message does not survive into it. The ring's halted label is `Resume download`: Apple's support article on stuck app downloads offered "Resume Download, Pause Download, or Cancel Download. If the app download is paused, tap Resume Download." (HT207165, archived 2019-12-16 at web.archive.org). The current iOS 27 menu text was not checked.

## Amendment (2026-09-24, #57): two fingers choose a run

The drawer's `FlatList` is wrapped in a `GestureDetector` whose two-finger pan chooses the run of rows under the fingers, in the download view and in Manage downloads, and takes `useSweep`'s list props: a `CellRendererComponent` that records each mounted row's place in the list's content, and the scroll, layout and content-size callbacks the edge scrolling needs. ## Amendment (2026-09-25, #64): several sentences of a chapter at once

The scheduler asked for one text, waited for its clip to be saved, and only then asked for the next. Measured with the owner's key on 2026-09-25 (`notes/NOTES_2026-09-25.md`, 13:27): Fish Audio's `s2.1-pro-free` answered a sentence in a median 2.76 s alone and 2.29 s with five out at once, so 40 sentences took 113.4 s one at a time and 21.2 s five at a time. Every reply carried `ratelimit-limit-concurrency: 5`, and none of the 360 was refused, up to ten at once.

Chapters are still written one at a time, in the order of the list. Inside the chapter being written, `write` in `scheduler.ts` keeps up to `concurrency(task)` of its texts requested at once. Each worker takes the next text and first makes the checks the single loop made before every text (`here()`, `allowed()`, `connected()`), then `exists`, `fetch` with the same bounded retry, and `changed`. A text a chapter holds twice is asked for once. The first worker that stops or fails stops the others taking more. A worker never rejects: whatever it throws, from `exists` or `changed` as much as from `fetch`, is recorded, so `write` settles only once every request out has settled. That keeps what the drawer and #56 rely on. `task.current` names one chapter, the next chapter's first request goes out after this chapter's last has settled, and the chapter's failure is recorded once, from the first failure, by the same `catch` as before. The first version awaited the workers with a `Promise.all` that rejected on the first throw while the others were still out. The failure would then have been handled, and the next chapter could have started, with requests of this chapter still in flight. A test in which `exists` throws for the fourth text while three requests are out is what showed it.

`concurrency` is the owner's **Sentences at once** for the task's provider: `AppSettings.sentencesAtOnce`, one number per provider, chosen from 1 to 10 on that provider's page in Settings › Providers. The first version (38e23dd) fixed it in the scheduler; the owner asked for it as a setting, and it is per provider because the limit is the service's. The defaults are the fixed values it replaced (`DEFAULT_SENTENCES_AT_ONCE`): Fish Audio 5, the limit its replies state and its documentation gives an account under $100 of lifetime spend; every other provider 1, so its downloads behave as before unless the owner raises it. A saved value the menu does not offer (0, 11, a string) reads as the provider's default, because 0 would stop every download. The scheduler asks for it once as each chapter starts, so a change during a download applies from the next chapter, and the row is not frozen while the provider is enabled: it changes nothing about the connection the switch checked. It is not in `engineIdentity`, since playback never reads it. Azure's free tier counts requests per minute (#40), and OpenAI was not measured. Measured afterwards (notes, 14:54), Azure, Kokoro and Chatterbox were four to six times as fast at five as at one with no `429`, and the defaults stayed one anyway: a free Azure tier or a small self-hosted server may refuse, and it is the owner's server to judge.

Speechify's provider queues its own requests (`sharedQueue`), so a width in the scheduler alone changed nothing: two at once measured 22.2 s against 23.3 s at one. The queue is now a `RequestQueue`: each request sent once fewer than its own width are out, and one at the head holds back everything behind it; a download's request joins the back, and any other goes ahead of the download's still waiting (#75, below). The offline runtime passes the download's number to the provider as `speechify.atOnce`, marked `speechify.download`; a reading goes through the same `synthesize` with neither, so a reading never sends more than one, and a reading's request that arrives during a download waits for the requests already out rather than for a slot. On the owner's plan anything above one is refused: at two and three, 17 and 32 replies were `429` with "your plan allows 1 simultaneous requests" and "1 request per second", and the audio delivered per second did not rise (notes, 15:09). The default stays one; the number exists for a plan that allows more. The task's own voice decides, not the voice reading now, as with everything else about a task. Eight and ten at once were answered too, but that is above the stated limit, which is why the default stays 5 and the menu stops at the 10 that was measured. If Fish enforced it, the extra requests would be refused and waited out by the provider (`Retry-After`, at most 5 s, three times), and a reading started while they were out would wait behind them.

Nothing about saving changed. `saveClip` already ran one at a time per document through `OfflineRepository.serialize`, catalogue writes go through the catalogue's single `writes` queue, and two requests for the same Speech Text share one flight in `synthesize`. A second save of the same key overwrites the file (`moveSync(…, { overwrite: true })`) and upserts the row.

When the scheduler is held back, up to five requests may be out where there was one. They finish and their clips are kept, as a paused chapter's request already was: `wanted()` in `fetch` looks at membership and deletion epochs, not at `allowed()`. A Reading no longer holds it back (#75, below): while a Reading plays, the download keeps its width and the reading's own requests go out beside its requests.

On the simulator (`notes/NOTES_2026-09-25.md`, 14:04), Chapters 101 and 102 of `My Vampire System 1-250`, 190 clips, took 115.92 s five at once and 560.85 s one at a time, from `enqueue` to the last clip saved, 4.84 times as fast. The five-at-once run also prepared Chapter 102's text, which the other did not need to. Its one long gap, 8.09 s, is the chapter boundary: the chapter's last requests settle, then the next chapter's text is prepared. Since #76 that text is normally prepared while the chapter before it is written, so the preparation is no longer part of the gap; the gap was not measured again. Writing the next chapter's first texts during that wait would remove it. It was not done, because it would change what the drawer and Manage downloads call the chapter being written (#38, #56).

## Amendment (2026-09-28, #76): chapter text is prepared ahead in the foreground, and never away from it

A download that reached a new chapter away from the screen stopped there. Measured on 2026-09-28 (`notes/NOTES_2026-09-28.md`, 02:49) on an iPhone 17 simulator with Fish `s2.1-pro-free` five at once: locked with nothing playing, the chapter being written finished 10.3 s after the lock, the task read `preparing` for the next chapter from 15 s and `blocked` from 71 s with "Chapter preparation timed out. Continue to try again."; locked while a Reading played, with #75's hold bypassed by `playbackActive(false)`, the app answered CDP for all 120 s and the Reading played on, and still the task read `preparing` from 5 s and `blocked` from 68 s. In the foreground beside the same Reading, the next chapter's text was prepared within one 5 s sample. Both locked runs were still `blocked` about 40 s after the app came back. The preparation is the hidden `DownloadIndexer` rendition: `runtime.ts` holds one `preparation` request, `indexer.tsx` injects `openreaderOfflineSection` and fails the request after 60 s, the scheduler's `catch` makes any error that is not a `SynthesisError` `blocked`, and only a tap (`tapChapter`, `resumeAll`) clears `blocked`. Away from the screen the rendition did not progress at all, so a longer limit would only have stopped later.

**One preparation path.** `prepare()` in `scheduler.ts` is the only way a chapter is prepared. Each request starts only once the one before it has settled, and one is kept per chapter in `preparations`, so the writer that needs a chapter being prepared ahead waits for that request rather than asking again. The runtime's single `preparation` slot, which a second request would overwrite, never holds two. As each request starts, the path checks `foreground()`, a new `SchedulerDeps` member that the runtime answers with `AppState.currentState === "active"`, the same test as its `foreground` flag, and throws `PreparationInterrupted` instead of asking. `inactive` counts as away: Control Center, the app switcher and the first step of locking all pass through it.

**Prepared ahead in the foreground.** `prepareAhead()` starts just before each `write` and whenever `run()` is called during a pass: the runtime's `kick` on returning to the foreground, after `enqueue`, after a ring tap. It prepares, one at a time, the unprepared chapters among the next `PREPARED_AHEAD` the writer will take. Those are in plan order, in the task, and not in the pass's `finished` (complete before it, or written in it), failed, paused or `task.current`. It sets neither `task.state` nor `task.current`, so the spinning ring stays on the chapter being written. It stops at `PreparationInterrupted` and starts again at the next `run()` in the foreground. It also stops at any other failure, which it remembers for the pass and leaves to the writer: when that chapter's turn comes the writer asks once more, and a second failure is recorded as before. Nothing on the screen shows a failure ahead, so it is logged once with `console.warn`, naming the chapter and the error's message. On the simulator one preparation ahead ended after 0.59 s with the app in front and its chapter not prepared, and nothing recorded why (`notes/NOTES_2026-09-28.md`, 05:01). A request that `cancelInactivePreparation` withdraws because the task stopped is not logged. A pass waits for `prepareAhead()` after the task's last `changed()`. By then `persist()`'s `cancelInactivePreparation` has withdrawn the request of a task that no longer runs.

**Never away from it.** The writer does not ask for a chapter whose `prepared` is false while `foreground()` is false. It throws `PreparationInterrupted` before setting `preparing`, and the `catch` makes the task `interrupted` with no error, or asks again at once if the app is already back. The runtime's AppState `active` handler already makes an `interrupted` task `queued`, so the download goes on without a tap. The drawer's line for `interrupted` is "Interrupted · continues when available", and its rings hold the triangle, as for `blocked` (#56).

**Abandoned, not failed.** The runtime registers its own `AppState` listener, apart from the background-task handler, which calls `abandonPreparation()` on any state other than `active`. That clears `preparation`, emits, and rejects with `PreparationInterrupted`. The indexer's `request` prop becomes null and its effect clears the 60 s timer. The token checks in `failPreparation` and `finishPreparation` turn a timer that fires anyway, or a late answer, into a no-op, as `test/offline/runtime-preparation.test.ts` checks against the real runtime. Every request's 60 s therefore runs in the foreground, and time away never counts against it. A preparation that fails or times out in the foreground is still a failure. `indexer.tsx` is unchanged.

**The bound.** `PREPARED_AHEAD` is 10. Without a bound, Select all on the owner's long document (2,077 chapters) would prepare every section back to back, up to about three hours at the 5 s or less measured for one, while the owner's page is in front. Each preparation renders a section in the hidden WebView, splits it into Utterances on the JavaScript thread in `sectionChapters` (the thread that runs the Player), computes membership keys (`saveSection` already yields every 128) and writes one exclusive transaction. With the bound, the first chapter written brings at most ten preparations back to back. After that it is one preparation for each chapter written, the same work at the same rate as before #76, done ten chapters earlier. At five at once a chapter of that document took about 58 s (190 clips in 115.92 s, #64 amendment), so ten chapters is about ten minutes of writing away from the screen, and about five times that at one at once. The reader's frame rate during the first ten was not measured. Away from the screen a download writes at most the chapters prepared ahead, and then waits as `interrupted` until the app returns. How long the app keeps running away from the screen is decided in #75 (beside a Reading) and #77 (background execution), not here.

## Amendment (2026-09-28, #75): a download goes on while a Reading plays

The scheduler's `allowed()` was `loaded && !storeError && !playing && (foreground || !expired)`, and `playing` was set by `playbackActive`, which an effect in `reading-view.tsx` called with `reading.status.playing`. Measured on 2026-09-28 (`notes/NOTES_2026-09-28.md`, 02:49) on an iPhone 17 simulator (iOS 27.0), a Debug build of 6d38e32, Fish Audio `s2.1-pro-free` at five at once and `My Vampire System 1-250.epub`, with `test/manual-test/download-away.cjs play`: the last clip was saved 2.4 s after Play and none in the remaining 18 s of the Reading, the task read `interrupted` from the first 5 s sample, and the first clip after Pause was saved 1.4 s later. The pass ended `interrupted` because `allowed()` was false at its next check. With the hold bypassed by calling `playbackActive(false)` once the Reading had started, a 60 s Reading and the download ran side by side: the Reading reported playing at every 5 s sample, the download saved 97 clips in those 62 s (about 1.6 a second, against 74 in the 42 s after Pause, about 1.8 a second), and the next chapter's text was prepared while the Reading played, between two samples.

The owner decided on 2026-09-28 that a download goes on while a Reading plays, in the foreground and with the phone locked, at the owner's own Sentences at once for the download's provider, not lowered while a Reading plays, and that the Reading keeps precedence wherever the app itself queues requests. `!playing` is gone from `allowed()`, and `playbackActive` and the reader's effect with it; nothing else read `playing`. Besides setting it, `playbackActive(false)` re-queued `interrupted` tasks while in the foreground and kicked the scheduler, on every change of `reading.status.playing` (the effect's cleanup ran before each new value) and on leaving the reader. The `interrupted` it undid were its own, passes that ended while `playing` was set. The others come from the background time running out, which the `AppState` `active` handler re-queues as before; from a restart, which `startDownloads` re-queues; and from a store failure, which makes `allowed()` false through `storeError`. Play and Pause no longer re-queue that last one; an owner who pressed neither never had them do it, and Resume all and coming back to the app still do.

Removing the hold was not enough with the phone locked, which was found by reading the code, not on a device. A Reading's audio keeps the app running away from the screen, but the bounded background task is a separate allowance, about half a minute on a phone (the simulator's was longer: `test/manual-test/recipes/drawers-library-and-probes.md`), and nothing in the app keeps it from expiring while the Reading plays. Its `expired` handler marked `preparing` and `downloading` tasks `interrupted`, and `(foreground || !expired)` would then have stopped the download in an app that was still running. So the runtime is told again whether a Reading plays, now to keep a download going: `setReadingPlays` sets `readingPlays`, `allowed()` is `loaded && !storeError && (foreground || !expired || readingPlays)`, and the `expired` handler interrupts nothing while it is set. When the Reading stops away from the screen after that time has run out, `setReadingPlays(false)` does what the handler would have done (`interruptWriting`: `preparing` and `downloading` become `interrupted`, and are persisted), and the return to the foreground re-queues them as before. When a Reading starts away from the screen after that time has run out, `interrupted` tasks are re-queued and the scheduler is kicked, so a Pause and a Play on the lock screen do not leave the download stopped until the app is opened; the issue's plan did not name that path, and it follows from the owner's "with the phone locked". This is the owner's own Reading, not audio played to extend execution: nothing is played for a download, and "No silent audio is used to extend execution" above still holds.

`ReadingHost` (`reading-host.tsx`) tells it, from `mine && live.playing`: the Reading's playing as `ReadingView` reports it through `onState`, for the Document held. The host is above the navigator and never unmounts, so leaving the Reader for the Library while the Reading plays (#68, ADR 0049) changes nothing, and its effect's cleanup tells `false` when the Reading pauses, another Document is opened or this one is deleted. An effect in `ReadingView` would also have held, since the view is moved rather than remounted when the Reader goes and unmounts only when the Reading ends, but the host is where the Reading is held, and `reading-host.test.ts` checks it there. `playing` includes buffering, when the engine goes on rendering silence in the playing state (footgun 3, `use-reading.ts`) and the audio session stays active. Whether the phone ends the background task while audio plays was not measured: in the simulator's `playlock` run, with the hold bypassed, the task went from `preparing` to `blocked` by the preparation timeout at 68 s, not to `interrupted`, so no expiration had arrived by then.

Speechify's `RequestQueue` is the one queue in the app that a Reading's requests and a download's share. The scheduler queues only a chapter's own texts, and every other provider sends each request as it is asked for, so there the Provider decides between the two. A request's width cannot say whose it is: Speechify's default Sentences at once is one, which is also every Reading's width. So `run` takes `download`, carried as `speechify.download` from the runtime's `synthesize`, which sets it, with `atOnce`, only when the scheduler's `fetch` passes the download's number. A download's request joins the back of the queue; any other goes in front of the first download request still waiting, behind the others that are not a download's. Nothing overtakes a request already out, and the head still holds back everything behind it, so a Reading's request, at width one, waits for what is out when it asks (at most the download's width and the Reading's own earlier request) and for nothing that asks after it. The voice list and the connection check are not marked either, and go ahead of a download too: the owner is waiting on them. One case keeps a download's place: `synthesize` shares one flight per document, voice and Speech Text, so a Reading that asks for a sentence the download has already asked for waits wherever that request stands in the queue. A download's request stands behind others only while a Reading's are ahead of it or its width is out; this was reasoned from the code, not measured.

What is given up: a provider that allows few requests at once or few a minute is now asked by both at once. Azure's free tier counts requests per minute (#40), and Speechify on the owner's plan takes one at a time and one a second. A Reading there may slow while a download runs, and a refusal is waited out by the provider before it reaches the Reading (Speechify: `Retry-After`, at most 5 s, three times; Azure: `RATE_LIMIT_WAITS_MS`, 1 s doubling to 16 s). Lowering the download to one at a time while a Reading plays was turned down by the owner: a Fish download would go five times slower whenever the owner listens, and at five beside the 60 s Reading above the Reading was not held up. Nothing about a provider's own limits was measured beside a Reading except Fish's.

Locked, the same bypass kept the app running for 120 s (`playlock`, notes 02:49), but the download stopped at the next chapter boundary, where the chapter's text preparation did not complete and timed out after 60 s; that is #76, and how long the phone lets a download run away from the screen when no Reading plays is #77. `test/offline/runtime-reading.test.ts` writes a queued download while a Reading's request is out, and checks the Speechify settings each request carries; it failed with `playbackActive(true)` called as the reader called it. The same file drives the app's state and the end of the background time: a download goes on past the expiration while a Reading plays (before `readingPlays`, it read `interrupted` at once), is `interrupted` as soon as the Reading stops after it and asks for nothing more, goes on when the app comes back, and goes on again when a Reading starts away from the screen after the expiration interrupted it. `test/app/reading-host.test.ts` checks what the host tells through a Reading played in the Reader, left for the Library, paused and played there, replaced by another Document and deleted while playing. The order test in `speechify.test.ts` has D1 out and D2, D3 waiting when R1 and R2 ask, and sends D1, R1, R2, D2, D3; before the change it sent D1, D2, D3, R1, R2.
